0. Writing answers that get full credit

These habits come from how your earlier quizzes were graded and from the 6(b) drafts on this project.

  1. Start with the answer. “Yes.” “Lasso.” “λ = 0.0058.” Graders shouldn’t have to infer your choice.
  2. Give one “because” sentence after it. A correct answer with no reason rarely gets full points.
  3. Cite the numbers you’re comparing. “Test MSE 1.8113 vs. 1.8274” beats “lasso is lower.”
  4. Don’t hedge your conclusion. “Might,” “potential,” and “suggests” stacked in one sentence read as no answer. Make the claim, then add a caveat if you need one.
  5. Answer every part of the question. 6(b) asked two things: why you’d be concerned, and whether there’s evidence. Number the parts in your head and check each one off.
  6. Check the direction. Lower MSE is better. Higher λ means more shrinkage.
  7. Don’t overclaim small gaps. Lasso beat OLS by 0.0161 in test MSE. That’s real but modest, so say so.
  8. Never leave a blank. A partly right sentence can get partial credit.

3. OLS, MSE, and overfitting

MSE in plain words

For every county, take actual minus predicted, square it, and average:

\[\text{MSE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_i - \hat{y}_i\right)^2\]

Squaring keeps misses above and below from canceling and punishes big misses more. The units are squared, so take the square root (RMSE) to get back to deathspc units. Your OLS test RMSE is √1.8274 ≈ 1.35.

A useful baseline: if you predicted the training mean for every county, your MSE would equal the variance of y (about 2.14 in training). Any model worth using has to beat that.

Training MSE vs. test MSE (SL deck, “Training vs. Test MSEs”)

Training MSE measures how well the model fits the counties it learned from. Test MSE measures how well it predicts counties it never saw. The slides say you can’t use training MSE to judge models because it’s biased toward overfitting.

Here’s the analogy. A student memorizes the answers to one practice exam, including its quirks. They ace that practice exam and then do worse on the real one. Training MSE is the practice score. Test MSE is the real exam.

Why overfitting was a concern here (Reg deck, “Big Data ⇒ New Challenges!”)

OLS does well when n is much bigger than p. Here OLS estimated 107 coefficients from 2,332 training counties, about one coefficient for every 22 counties. Each coefficient is another knob OLS can turn to shrink training error, even when the pattern is only noise.

Some knobs rest on almost no data. Delaware has 2 counties in the training set, so its dummy can basically fit those 2 counties’ death rates, noise included.

The extreme version is von Neumann’s elephant from the slides: with as many parameters as data points, the model is saturated. In-sample fit is perfect and out-of-sample fit is terrible.

Your evidence

OLS 1.2867 1.8274

Test MSE is about 42% higher than training MSE, which is evidence of overfitting. Part of the gap comes from the test counties being more spread out (variance 2.55 vs. 2.14), but the gap persists after adjusting: the model leaves 60% of the variance unexplained in training (1.29 / 2.14) and 72% in test (1.83 / 2.55).

4. Ridge and lasso: the theory

The idea in one sentence

Ridge and lasso fit the same linear model as OLS but add a penalty for big coefficients, so the model can’t chase noise as hard.

The ELI5 version: OLS gets unlimited money to buy coefficients. Ridge and lasso charge a price for each one, so the model only “buys” a big coefficient when it really pays off in fit.

The formulas (Reg deck, “RR & The Lasso Estimation”)

OLS minimizes: \[\text{RSS} = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(y_i - \beta_0 - \sum_{j=1}^{p}\beta_j x_{ij}\Big)^2\]

Ridge minimizes: \[\text{RSS} + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p}\beta_j^2 \qquad (\ell_2 \text{ penalty, squared})\]

Lasso minimizes: \[\text{RSS} + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p}|\beta_j| \qquad (\ell_1 \text{ penalty})\]

The intercept β₀ is not penalized. The RSS term pulls coefficients toward fitting the training data, and the penalty pulls them toward zero. λ sets how hard the penalty pulls.

What λ does

λ Ridge Lasso
λ = 0 Exactly OLS Exactly OLS
Small λ Slight shrinkage Slight shrinkage, a few coefficients become exactly 0
Large λ Everything shrunk close to 0, but never exactly 0 Many coefficients exactly 0 (a sparse model)
λ → ∞ All slopes → 0, predicts ≈ the mean All slopes = 0, predicts the mean

λ is a hyperparameter. You choose it before fitting, and it can’t be chosen by in-sample fit. The βs are parameters, which are estimated by fitting.

Why lasso makes exact zeros and ridge doesn’t (Reg deck, “The Lasso vs. RR Picture”)

Rewrite each as “minimize RSS subject to a budget.” Ridge’s budget is β₁² + β₂² ≤ s, a circle. Lasso’s budget is |β₁| + |β₂| ≤ s, a diamond with corners sitting on the axes.

The RSS contours are ellipses that grow outward from the OLS estimate until they first touch the budget region. A diamond’s corners stick out, so the first touch is often at a corner, where one coefficient is exactly 0. A circle has no corners, so the touch point almost never lands exactly on an axis.

That’s why the slides say lasso performs variable selection and ridge doesn’t.

Why accepting bias can improve prediction (Reg deck, “Why Do RR/The Lasso Improve Over OLS?”)

Recall from SL that expected test MSE = variance + bias² + irreducible error. The penalty reduces flexibility, so bias goes up and variance goes down. If OLS’s variance is high (n not much bigger than p, or lots of correlated predictors), the drop in variance outweighs the added bias and test MSE falls.

This also explains why training MSE always gets worse with a penalty. OLS already found the coefficients with the lowest possible training RSS, so any shrinkage moves away from that. Your results show it: ridge and lasso had higher training MSE than OLS but lower test MSE.

Ridge vs. lasso: which wins? (Reg deck, “Comparing RR & the Lasso”)

Neither always wins. Ridge tends to do better when the outcome depends on many predictors with small effects. Lasso tends to do better when it depends on relatively few. You never know which in advance, so you use cross-validation to decide.

Elastic net mixes both penalties and adds a second hyperparameter α for the mix.

5. Scaling

Why scale before ridge and lasso (Reg deck, “Standardized Coefficients”)

OLS is scale equivariant. If you measure income in thousands instead of dollars, its coefficient just gets multiplied by 1,000, and predictions don’t change.

Ridge and lasso aren’t. The penalty depends on the size of the coefficient, and that size depends on the units. median_house_value is in dollars (up to about 1.3 million), so its coefficient is tiny and barely gets penalized. Measured in millions, its coefficient would be bigger and get penalized more. Standardizing puts every predictor in standard-deviation units, so the penalty treats them all fairly.

Standardizing means subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation, which gives each predictor mean 0 and variance 1.

Fit the scaler on training data only

scaler = StandardScaler()
X_train_s = scaler.fit_transform(X_train)   # learn mean/sd from TRAIN, apply to train
X_test_s  = scaler.transform(X_test)        # reuse TRAIN mean/sd on test

Calling fit on the test set would let test information leak into training. The assignment’s footnote says to scale after splitting for this reason.

The scaled-data trap

Ridge and lasso were fit on X_train_s, so their coefficients expect standardized inputs. In your first Q8 run, predicting on unscaled X_test gave a ridge MSE of about 32 million. The clue was sklearn’s warning, “X has feature names, but Ridge was fitted without feature names.” It meant the model was trained on a plain array and was now getting a DataFrame.

6. Tuning λ with 10-fold CV

The algorithm (Reg deck, “RR & the Lasso Tuning Algorithm”)

  1. Choose a grid of λ values.
  2. For each λ, estimate the model and compute the CV error.
  3. Pick the λ with the smallest CV error.
  4. Refit the model with that λ on all the training data.

How k-fold CV works (Res deck, slides 31 to 33)

  1. Randomly divide the training data into k folds (you used 10).
  2. Fit the model on k − 1 folds.
  3. Predict the held-out fold and compute its MSE.
  4. Repeat until each fold has been held out once.
  5. Average the k MSEs. That average estimates the test error.

From the Res deck: k-fold CV sits between LOOCV and the validation-set approach. It’s less biased and less noisy than a single validation split, and much cheaper than LOOCV. In practice, k = 5 or 10.

Your code

exponents_lasso = np.linspace(-4, 0, 101)   # 101 evenly spaced exponents
alphas_lasso = 10 ** exponents_lasso         # 0.0001 ... 1 on a log scale

folds = KFold(n_splits=10, shuffle=True, random_state=25)

cv_mse_lasso = []
for a in alphas_lasso:
    scores = cross_val_score(Lasso(alpha=a, max_iter=10000), X_train_s, y_train,
                             cv=folds, scoring='neg_mean_squared_error')
    cv_mse_lasso.append(-scores.mean())     # flip sign, average the 10 folds

best_alpha_lasso = alphas_lasso[np.argmin(cv_mse_lasso)]
lasso_best = Lasso(alpha=best_alpha_lasso, max_iter=10000).fit(X_train_s, y_train)
Piece What it does
np.linspace(a, b, 101) 101 evenly spaced numbers from a to b, including both ends
10 ** exponents Spaces λ evenly on a log scale, so small and large values get equal coverage
KFold(..., shuffle=True, random_state=25) Instructions for cutting the data into folds. It doesn’t cut anything until it’s used
cross_val_score Runs the fit-and-predict loop over all 10 folds and returns 10 scores
'neg_mean_squared_error' sklearn treats bigger scores as better, so it reports negative MSE. That’s why you flip the sign
np.argmin Position of the smallest CV error
max_iter=10000 Lasso is solved iteratively and may not converge at small λ with the default 1,000

Two seeds, two jobs

random_state=11 controls the train/test split. random_state=25 controls the CV folds inside the training set. Mixing them up changes your results.

Why λ is never picked with the test set

The test set is your honest final exam. If you used it to choose λ, you’d be tuning to those particular 583 counties, and the test MSE would be optimistic. It’s the same reason training MSE can’t be used.

Training MSE can’t choose λ either, because the penalty only ever raises training RSS. Training MSE would always pick λ = 0, which is just OLS.

A minimum on the edge of the grid

Your starting grid (0.01 to 100) put ridge’s minimum at the right edge and lasso’s at the left edge. A minimum on the edge means the true best λ is probably outside the grid, so you widen it in that direction and rerun until the curve bottoms out in the middle. You moved ridge to 10⁰ through 10⁴ and lasso to 10⁻⁴ through 10⁰.

Why ridge’s λ is so much bigger than lasso’s

sklearn writes the two losses differently. Ridge uses RSS + α‖β‖², while Lasso uses RSS / (2n) + α‖β‖₁. Dividing by 2n shrinks lasso’s RSS term, so its α values live on a much smaller scale. You can’t compare λ_ridge = 208.93 with λ_lasso = 0.0058 and conclude ridge penalizes harder.

Reading the lasso CV plot

For λ above about 0.5, the lasso curve goes flat at around 2.14. That number is roughly the variance of y_train: every coefficient has been shrunk to exactly 0, so the model predicts the mean for every county. Ridge’s curve never flattens like that, because ridge never sets coefficients exactly to 0.

9. Practice questions

Answer each one out loud or on paper before opening the answer.

Concepts

1. What does λ control in ridge and lasso? What do you get at λ = 0 and as λ → ∞?

TipAnswer

λ controls how strongly the penalty pulls coefficients toward zero compared with fitting the training data. At λ = 0, both are exactly OLS. As λ → ∞, all slope coefficients go to 0 (exactly 0 for lasso, approaching 0 for ridge), and the model predicts the mean of y.

2. Why can lasso set coefficients exactly to zero when ridge can’t?

TipAnswer

Lasso’s constraint region (|β₁| + |β₂| ≤ s) is a diamond with corners on the axes, and the RSS ellipses often first touch it at a corner, where one coefficient is exactly 0. Ridge’s region (β₁² + β₂² ≤ s) is a circle with no corners, so the touch point almost never lands exactly on an axis. So lasso does variable selection and ridge doesn’t.

3. Which coefficient vector gets the bigger ridge penalty, β = (3, 4) or β = (2, 5)? Which gets the bigger lasso penalty?

TipAnswer

Ridge: 3² + 4² = 25 vs. 2² + 5² = 29, so (2, 5) is penalized more, because squaring punishes the single big coefficient. Lasso: |3| + |4| = 7 vs. |2| + |5| = 7, so they’re tied.

4. Ridge and lasso add bias. How can that lower test MSE?

TipAnswer

Test MSE depends on bias² plus variance. The penalty makes the model less flexible, which raises bias but lowers variance. When OLS’s variance is high, the drop in variance is bigger than the rise in bias², so test MSE goes down.

5. When would you expect ridge to beat lasso, and the other way around?

TipAnswer

Ridge tends to win when the outcome depends on many predictors with small effects. Lasso tends to win when only a few predictors matter. Since you don’t know which is true beforehand, you compare their cross-validated errors.

6. Why can’t you choose λ by picking the value with the lowest training MSE?

TipAnswer

OLS already minimizes training RSS, and the penalty can only move the fit away from that. Training MSE would always choose λ = 0, which is just OLS. λ has to be chosen by an estimate of test error, like cross-validation.

7. Why is λ tuned with cross-validation on the training set instead of with the test set?

TipAnswer

If you used the test set to pick λ, you’d be tuning to those specific counties, and the test MSE would be an optimistic, contaminated estimate. Keeping the test set untouched until the end gives an honest measure of how the final model performs on new data.

Code

8. Why do you scale predictors before ridge and lasso, and why fit the scaler only on the training data?

TipAnswer

The penalty depends on coefficient size, and coefficient size depends on each variable’s units, so without scaling, some predictors would be penalized far more than others for no real reason. Standardizing puts them all in standard-deviation units. You fit the scaler on training data only so that no information from the test set leaks into training.

9. What does cross_val_score(..., cv=folds, scoring='neg_mean_squared_error') return, and why is there a minus sign in -scores.mean()?

TipAnswer

It returns 10 numbers, one per fold, each the negative MSE on that held-out fold. sklearn uses negative MSE because it treats bigger scores as better. Flipping the sign and averaging gives the 10-fold CV estimate of test MSE for that λ.

10. Your ridge CV plot shows the lowest error at λ = 100, the largest value in your grid. What does that tell you, and what do you do?

TipAnswer

The best λ is probably larger than 100, outside the grid, so the curve hasn’t reached its minimum yet. Extend the grid upward (for example, 10⁰ to 10⁴), rerun the CV, and check that the minimum now sits in the middle of the curve.

11. A classmate’s ridge test MSE is 32,650,167 while OLS is 1.83. What’s the most likely bug?

TipAnswer

They fit ridge on scaled data but predicted on unscaled X_test. The coefficients expect standardized inputs, so raw values like house prices in the hundreds of thousands blow up the predictions. The fix is to predict on X_test_s.

12. You kept all 47 state dummies plus an intercept in OLS. Why did it still run, and what can’t you interpret?

TipAnswer

The 47 dummies sum to 1 in every row, which duplicates the intercept (perfect collinearity). sklearn still finds one of the many equally good least-squares solutions, and they all give the same predictions, so the MSEs are valid. But the individual state coefficients aren’t uniquely identified, so you can’t interpret them.

13. What’s the difference between random_state=11 and random_state=25 in this project?

TipAnswer

random_state=11 fixes the 80/20 train/test split. random_state=25 fixes how the training data is shuffled into 10 CV folds. Both make the results reproducible, but they control different random steps.

14. The prompt mentions 49 state dummies. Why did you end up with 47?

TipAnswer

After dropping rows with missing values, no counties remained for DC or New Jersey, so get_dummies had no category to create for them.

15. True or false: since λ_ridge = 208.93 and λ_lasso = 0.0058, ridge is penalizing far more heavily. Explain.

TipAnswer

False. sklearn’s Lasso divides the RSS by 2n while Ridge doesn’t, so their λ values are on different scales. The penalties also have different forms (sum of squares vs. sum of absolute values), so the raw λ values can’t be compared across the two models.

Interpretation

16. OLS has a training MSE of 1.2867 and a test MSE of 1.8274. Write a full-credit answer to “Why might you be concerned about overfitting, and is there evidence of it?”

TipAnswer

Overfitting is a concern because OLS estimates 107 coefficients from only 2,332 training counties, and some state dummies rest on very few counties (Delaware has 2), so the model has enough flexibility to fit noise. Yes, there is evidence of overfitting: test MSE (1.8274) is about 42% higher than training MSE (1.2867), so the model predicts the counties it was fit on much better than new ones. The gap remains after accounting for the test set’s higher variance (60% of the variance unexplained in training vs. 72% in test).

17. In the lasso CV plot, the error goes flat at about 2.14 for large λ. Why that value?

TipAnswer

At large λ, lasso sets every slope coefficient to exactly 0, so the model predicts the training mean for every county. The MSE of predicting the mean is the variance of y, which is about 2.14 in your training data.

18. Lasso set the coefficient on tax_st_diff_top20 to 0. Does that mean state tax progressivity has no relationship with COVID deaths?

TipAnswer

No. tax_st_diff_top20 has the same value for every county in a state, so the state dummies already capture it completely, and lasso doesn’t need it for prediction. A zero lasso coefficient means a variable isn’t needed for prediction given the others. It says nothing about whether the factor is related to deaths.

19. Did ridge and lasso improve on OLS? By how much, and why might the gains be small here?

TipAnswer

Yes, both did. Ridge lowered test MSE from 1.8274 to 1.8209 (about 0.4%), and lasso lowered it to 1.8113 (about 0.9%). The gains are modest because n is much larger than p (about 22 training counties per coefficient), so OLS’s variance wasn’t extreme and shrinkage had limited room to help.

20. Three counties have actual deathspc values of 1, 0, and 3, and predictions of 1.5, 0.5, and 2. Compute the MSE.

TipAnswer

Errors: 1 − 1.5 = −0.5, 0 − 0.5 = −0.5, 3 − 2 = 1. Squared: 0.25, 0.25, 1. MSE = 1.5 / 3 = 0.5. RMSE = √0.5 ≈ 0.71.

21. Which model would you recommend to the CDC, and why?

TipAnswer

Lasso, because it has the lowest test MSE (1.8113 vs. 1.8209 for ridge and 1.8274 for OLS), so it predicts new counties best, which is what the CDC needs to target vaccines. It also drops 16 predictors, which makes the model somewhat simpler to explain. The improvement over OLS is modest (about 0.9%), so OLS isn’t a bad model, but lasso is the best of the three.
