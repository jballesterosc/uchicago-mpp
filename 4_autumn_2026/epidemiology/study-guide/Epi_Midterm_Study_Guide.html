2 Exam rules & answer-writing habits

The exam instructions say short answers can be “just a few words,” write legibly, show work for partial credit, basic calculator only (no phone). In the sample midterm, answers written only inside a table got no partial credit — always copy the final number into the answer space.

Habits that earn points:

  1. Formula → numbers → answer → units. Write CI = a/(a+b) = 329/252,240 = 130.4 per 100,000 rather than just the final number. Units are part of the answer (e.g., “3.42 deaths per 100,000 per year”).
  2. Ratio interpretation template: “[Exposed group] had [X] times the [risk/rate/odds] of [outcome] compared with [unexposed group] over [time period].” Always say who is compared to whom.
  3. OR → say “odds,” never “risk.”
  4. CI for a ratio: if it includes 1 → not statistically significant. For a difference: if it includes 0 → not significant.
  5. “Internal or external validity?” Problems before randomization (exclusions, refusals) → external. Problems after randomization (loss to follow-up, noncompliance, crossovers) → internal.
  6. Keep conceptual answers to a phrase or two. Graders are hunting for keywords — make them easy to find.

In screening: a = true positives, b = false positives, c = false negatives, d = true negatives.

4 Calculation playbooks (one per HW skill)

Incidence / prevalence / mortality “goes up, down, or same?” (PSET 1 Q1, PSET 3 screening h)

Reason from P ≈ I × D and from what each measure’s numerator and denominator count.

Intervention Incidence Prevalence Cause-specific mortality Case-fatality
Cure (e.g., new HCV drugs) Same (doesn’t stop new infections) ↓ (duration shortens) ↓ ↓
Treatment that prolongs life without cure Same ↑ (duration lengthens) ↓ ↓
Prevention (vaccine, risk-factor change) ↓ ↓ (eventually) ↓ Usually same
New screening program ↑ diagnosed incidence (earlier detection + overdiagnosis) ↑ ↓ only if early treatment works ↓ (milder cases added to denominator)
Broader case definition (e.g., 2017 hypertension 130/80) ↑ ↑ Depends on how deaths are coded ↓

Person-time and incidence rates (PSET 1 Q2, Q4)

Each person contributes time from entry until the first of: the event, loss to follow-up, death from another cause, or the end of the study. Rate = events ÷ total person-time. Trap: these are rates (denominator = person-time), not cumulative incidences (denominator = people). Always write the units: “472.1 per 100,000 person-years.”

Smallest/largest possible person-time questions: smallest is ~0 (event or exit right after entry); largest is from the earliest possible entry date to the end of follow-up.

Direct age adjustment (PSET 1 Q3; sample midterm melanoma)

  1. Age-specific rate in each group = cases ÷ population × 100,000.
  2. Expected cases = age-specific rate × standard population in that age group ÷ 100,000.
  3. Add up expected cases across age groups.
  4. Adjusted rate = total expected ÷ total standard population × 100,000.
  5. Compare adjusted rates only if they used the same standard.

Interpreting crude vs. adjusted (for a disease that rises with age): if crude > adjusted, the study population is older than the standard; if crude < adjusted, it’s younger. If the crude and adjusted comparisons tell the same story, the populations’ age structures don’t differ much; if they flip, age was distorting the crude comparison. The adjusted rate itself is a fictional number — it only exists for comparison.

Why adjust (4-point answer from PSET 1): age is a strong predictor of disease/death and populations differ in age structure; applying each population’s age-specific rates to one standard removes the effect of age differences so the comparison is fair.

Indirect adjustment / SMR (PSET 1 Q2c; Framingham article; mustard-gas example)

Use when the study population is small (age-specific rates unstable): occupational, environmental, or small cohorts.

  1. Take age-specific rates from a standard population (e.g., national rates).
  2. Multiply by the study population’s count in each age group → expected deaths.
  3. SMR = observed ÷ expected.

Interpretation: “Mortality among [study group] was 5.82 times what would be expected if they had the [standard population]’s age-specific death rates.” SMRs from two different study populations shouldn’t be compared directly (different age structures).

Outbreak attack-rate table (PSET 2)

  1. For each food: AR_ate = ill who ate ÷ all who ate; AR_didn’t = ill who didn’t eat ÷ all who didn’t eat.
  2. RR = AR_ate ÷ AR_didn’t.
  3. Culprit = high AR among eaters, low AR among non-eaters, highest RR, and most ill people ate it.
  4. Odds of illness among eaters = ill ÷ not ill (among eaters).

Classic follow-ups: Cases among non-eaters? Cross-contamination (shared utensils), misreported/forgotten exposure (recall), another source. (For staph, illness comes from preformed toxin in food, so person-to-person spread is not the explanation.) Why might a food look protective (RR < 1)? Confounding — people who filled up on that food may have eaten less of the culprit — or chance. Protective RR range: 0 ≤ RR < 1.

Kaplan-Meier (PSET 3 Q2; sample midterm figure)

  1. Sort all times; mark censored with +. At ties, list the event before the censored time (the censored person was still at risk).
  2. Only compute a row at event times. At risk = everyone still being observed just before that time (subtract earlier events and earlier censorings).
  3. Conditional survival = 1 − (events ÷ at risk).
  4. Cumulative S(t) = previous S × conditional survival.
  5. Median survival = first time S(t) ≤ 0.50. If the curve never drops to 0.50, the median is not reached (this was the answer for the vitamin D figure — less than 7% got cancer).
  6. Graph: step function, y-axis “cumulative probability of remaining event-free,” x-axis time; cumulative incidence = 1 − S(t).

Trap: never divide by the original N after someone has been censored — that’s the whole point of KM.

Assumptions: censored people have the same prognosis as those still followed (non-informative censoring); no secular trend in risk across the enrollment period. Log-rank test H0: the survival curves are the same in all groups over the entire follow-up.

Screening 2×2 (PSET 3; sample midterm Pap vs HPV)

Building the table from Se, Sp, and prevalence (the PSA problem):

  1. Diseased = N × prevalence; non-diseased = N − diseased.
  2. a = Se × diseased; c = diseased − a.
  3. d = Sp × non-diseased; b = non-diseased − d.
  4. Fill in row totals, then PPV = a/(a+b), NPV = d/(c+d).

Decision rules: choose higher sensitivity when missing a case is costly — the disease is serious, early treatment saves lives, and positives can be confirmed (this is why HPV beats Pap in the sample midterm). Choose higher specificity when false positives are costly — invasive or risky follow-up, anxiety, labeling. Raising prevalence (screening a higher-risk group, or retesting only the positives) raises PPV. Lowering the positivity threshold raises Se and lowers Sp.

Names matter: if asked “what is the exact name of the metric,” write the name (“prevalence,” “sensitivity”) — not a description or formula.

Cohort measures: RR, AR, AR%, PAR, PAR% (PSET 4)

Work in incidence units consistently (convert to per 100,000 at the end). Interpretation sentences (IDA → transfusion example):

Measure One-sentence interpretation
RR = 87 Pregnancies with IDA had 87 times the risk of transfusion during delivery hospitalization compared with pregnancies without IDA.
AR = 128.9 per 100,000 Among IDA pregnancies, about 129 transfusions per 100,000 are attributable to IDA (if causal).
AR% = 98.9% Among IDA pregnancies, about 99% of transfusions are attributable to IDA.
PAR% = 89.8% Among all pregnancies, about 90% of transfusions are attributable to IDA.

PAR% < AR% because exposure isn’t universal — unexposed cases dilute the population’s attributable share. PAR also = AR × prevalence of exposure.

Efficacy and NNT (Salk lecture; BNT162b2 article; LEAP “relative reduction”)

Efficacy = (R_control − R_treated) ÷ R_control. Salk paralytic polio, placebo areas: (57 − 16)/57 = 72%; observed-control areas: (46 − 17)/46 = 63%. NNT = 1 ÷ (difference in rates as proportions): 1 ÷ (41/100,000) ≈ 2,439 children vaccinated to prevent one case of paralytic polio. NNT is large when the disease is rare even if efficacy is high — it depends on baseline risk; efficacy doesn’t.

Odds ratio (Lecture 10; PSET 2 odds; Semmelweis discussion)

OR = ad/bc. You can’t compute incidence or RR in a case-control study because the investigator chose how many cases and controls to enroll. The OR approximates the RR when the disease is rare (< ~10%) and is always farther from 1 than the RR. Zika example: (24 × 22)/(39 × 6) = 2.26 → “the odds of maternal Zika infection were 2.26 times higher among microcephaly cases than controls.”

Quick ones: herd immunity, kappa

Herd immunity threshold = 1 − 1/R0 (R0 = 5 → 80% immune). Assumes random mixing and complete immunity. Kappa: compute observed agreement, then chance agreement from each rater’s marginal positive rate (chance both + = P(A+) × P(B+); chance both − = P(A−) × P(B−)), then κ = (obs − chance)/(1 − chance).

Stats interpretation (Week 1 section; PSET 1 Q5)

Concept Correct wording
p-value If H0 were true, the probability of observing data as extreme or more extreme than what was observed. (Not the probability H0 is true.)
95% CI If we repeated the sampling many times, 95% of the intervals constructed would contain the true value. Practical reading: plausible range for the true value; if a ratio’s CI excludes 1, significant at α = 0.05.
Regression β “For each 1-unit increase in X, Y is on average β units higher, adjusting for the other variables in the model.”
Significant vs. not The difference between “significant” and “not significant” is not itself statistically significant — compare estimates directly.

5 Concept review by lecture (high-yield contrasts)

L1 — What epidemiology is; study designs

Epidemiology studies how health and disease are distributed in populations and the factors that determine that distribution; the unit is the population, it’s quantitative, and the goal is prevention. Descriptive epi asks how many, who, where, when (burden, surveillance, natural history). Analytic epi asks why (etiology) and whether interventions work.

Design Who decides exposure? Direction / measure Strength Weakness
RCT Investigator, by chance Forward; RR, efficacy Controls known & unknown confounders Expensive; ethics; external validity
Before/after (historical control) Investigator Compare periods Simple Anything else that changed over time
Cohort People themselves Forward from exposure; RR, AR Temporality; multiple outcomes Cost, loss to follow-up, confounding; rare outcomes need huge N
Case-control People themselves Backward from disease; OR Rare diseases, fast, cheap Control selection, recall bias
Cross-sectional People themselves Same time; prevalence Quick snapshot Can’t establish temporality
Ecologic Groups Group-level rates Cheap, hypothesis-generating Ecologic fallacy
Natural (quasi-) experiment Circumstances outside people’s choice Usually cohort-like Approximates randomization Assignment rarely truly random

Goldberger & pellagra (lecture example of every design): case series (institution inmates got pellagra, staff never did → not contagious; diet differed); before/after diet experiment in two orphanages (cases nearly vanished, but no concurrent control); cohort in 7 cotton-mill villages (income inversely related to incidence, still true after age adjustment); ecologic village comparison; qualitative study (village “In” had no fresh-food market — what we’d now call a food desert). Lesson: individual and contextual factors both matter.

Descriptive data sources: vital records (births, deaths), notifiable disease surveillance (complete for rabies/plague, less for gonorrhea), registries (SEER), surveys (NHANES), administrative/claims data, health-system clinical data. Always ask how the data were generated and who is missing.

L2 — Measures, case definitions, age adjustment

Count vs. proportion (0–1, numerator in denominator) vs. rate (time in denominator) vs. ratio. Case definitions change with discovery, technology, and politics — and create artificial trends: the 2017 hypertension threshold (140/90 → 130/80), staggered pregnancy checkboxes on death certificates (apparent rise in maternal mortality), successive DSM autism definitions, evolving AIDS case definitions (manifestation → manifestation + etiology).

L3 — Infectious disease & outbreaks

Term Meaning
Endemic Always present at a habitual level (TB in US)
Sporadic Appears infrequently, no habitual level (rabies after control)
Epidemic Occurrence in excess of expectation
Pandemic Worldwide epidemic
Outbreak Sudden increase, usually single exposure/common vehicle
Seasonal vs. secular trend Annual pattern vs. multi-year trend

Epidemiologic triad: host, agent, environment (± vector). Transmission: direct (respiratory, enteric, sexual, skin, blood, fomites) vs. indirect (common vehicle: water, food, air; vector). Iceberg: visible cases are the tip; subclinical, preclinical, chronic, and carrier infections (Typhoid Mary) drive transmission. Polio: ~70–75% asymptomatic, <1% paralytic.

SIR model: susceptible → infectious → recovered/resistant; public health either reduces susceptibles (vaccination) or reduces mixing. R0 = average secondary cases from one case in a fully susceptible population; not a fixed constant (measles estimates 5–18). Rt = under current conditions. >1 grows, =1 stable, <1 declines. Overdispersion → super-spreaders.

Outbreak investigation steps: (1) define a case; (2) describe cases by time and place (epidemic curve); (3) gather exposure information; (4) calculate attack rates; (5) test hypotheses by comparing attack rates; (6) recommend control and prevention measures. Raw-milk Salmonella example: lab + WGS case definition, 171 cases, 70% drank brand A vs 1.9% expected → recall.

John Snow: Broad Street outbreak (interviews, spot map, pump handle removed Sept 8, 1854 — after the outbreak was already waning) and the natural experiment of Southwark & Vauxhall (downstream intake with sewage) vs. Lambeth (moved upstream in 1852), ~300,000 people assigned to water source without choice, on the same streets. Farr’s elevation analysis supported miasma theory — elevation was confounded with water source.

L5 — Survival analysis & life expectancy

Survival methods handle when events happen and censoring (lost to follow-up, death from other causes, study end). KM uses exact event times; life tables group into intervals; Cox models adjust for confounders and give hazard ratios (read like RRs). Life expectancy = mean age at death of a hypothetical cohort that lives its whole life at this year’s age-specific death rates. LE at 65 plus 65 exceeds LE at birth because it’s conditional on having survived to 65.

L6 — Screening

Screening finds disease in asymptomatic people (low pre-test probability); diagnosis confirms disease in people with symptoms (high pre-test probability). Same test, different PPV.

Prevention levels: primary (prevent onset), secondary (detect pre-symptomatic disease — screening), tertiary (prevent worse outcomes after diagnosis).

Screening is worthwhile when: long preclinical phase; cheap, low-risk test; positives can be confirmed; early treatment improves outcomes and is available.

Harms: direct (colonoscopy perforation), false positives (cost, risky follow-up, anxiety, labeling), false negatives (false reassurance), overdiagnosis.

Bias What it does
Lead-time bias Diagnosis moved earlier → survival after diagnosis looks longer even if the death date doesn’t change
Length bias Screening preferentially catches slow-growing disease (long preclinical phase) with better prognosis
Overdiagnosis Detects disease that would never have caused symptoms or death

So the right outcome for evaluating a screening program is cause-specific mortality in screened vs unscreened populations — not incidence, and not survival after diagnosis.

Reliability vs validity: reliability = consistency (inter-rater, intra-rater, test-retest); validity = accuracy (Se, Sp). A test can be reliably wrong. Kappa measures agreement beyond chance.

L8 — Cohort studies

Feature Options
Timing Prospective (enroll now, follow forward) / Historical (records link past exposure to outcomes that already occurred) / Mixed
Follow-up Fixed (same calendar time → cumulative incidence) / Dynamic (varying time → incidence rate, survival)
Enrollment Exposure-based (radium dial painters, Dutch Hunger Winter) / General population (Framingham = geographic; British Doctors = “opportunistic” roster)

Call the comparison group “unexposed,” not “controls.” Advantages: temporality, multiple outcomes, power grows with follow-up. Disadvantages: time and money, loss to follow-up (internal validity), huge N for rare outcomes, selection bias/confounding.

Classics: Radium dial painters (historical exposure-based; radium ingestion → bone cancers). Dutch Hunger Winter (sharply bounded famine in a well-nourished population; first-trimester exposure → ~2.5× schizophrenia in women). Framingham (1948; random sample of 2/3 of adults 30–62, 69% response; exams every 2 years; smoking, cholesterol, BP, inactivity, obesity → CHD; Framingham Risk Score). British Doctors (Doll & Hill 1951, 2/3 of UK doctors responded; dose-response between smoking and lung cancer).

L9 — Confounding

A confounder is a third variable that is (1) associated with the exposure, (2) a cause of the outcome, and (3) not on the causal pathway. A variable on the pathway is a mediator (HBV → cirrhosis → liver cancer; late bedtime → snacking → weight gain; light drinking → HDL → less heart disease). Adjusting for a mediator removes part of the real effect.

Pattern Meaning
Stratum RRs similar to each other but different from crude Confounding (adjust; report M-H RR)
Stratum RRs differ from each other Interaction / effect modification (report stratum-specific RRs; there is a test for this)
Crude farther from 1 than adjusted Positive confounding (IDA example: crude 87 → adjusted 8.2)
Crude closer to 1 than adjusted Negative confounding
Direction flips Qualitative confounding

There’s no statistical test for confounding — it’s a judgment comparing crude and adjusted estimates. Control in design: randomization, restriction (cannabis study restricted to non-tobacco users), matching. Control in analysis: stratification with Mantel-Haenszel weighted averages (hypertension → stroke: crude 3.2, age-adjusted 2.4), regression. Residual confounding: categories too broad or poor proxy measures (insurance type for SES).

Confounder checklist (PSET 4 e): for each candidate, explicitly answer all three criteria. Example: number of prenatal visits — associated with IDA, but partly caused by the IDA diagnosis (more visits after diagnosis) → on the pathway/consequence of exposure → don’t adjust.

L10 — Case-control studies

Start with cases (incident cases preferred — prevalent cases mix incidence with survival) and controls who would have become cases in your study had they developed the disease (population cases → population controls; clinic cases → clinic controls). Hospital controls: easy and comparable recall, but may share unrecognized risk factors (smoking, coffee) — use acute and varied conditions. Friend/neighbor controls risk overmatching.

Matching controls confounding in design but: you can’t study the matched variable’s effect, you must use matched analyses, and 1:1 concordant pairs waste information (use >1 control per case). Biases: selection bias (control ascertainment related to exposure; low control response), recall bias (cases search memory harder — fix with diseased controls, prompt interviews, validation, dummy risk factors), observation/interviewer bias (blind interviewers, standardized questionnaires). Non-differential misclassification of a binary exposure usually biases toward the null; differential misclassification can bias in either direction.

Examples: Zika/microcephaly (OR 2.26) and SIDS (19 case-control studies, ORs 1.2–14 for prone sleeping; RCT considered inappropriate; national campaigns → UK cot deaths halved 1991–92).

6 Required articles — design, design issues, big-picture result

No numbers to memorize. For each, be able to say in ~60 seconds: the question, the design, two or three design issues, and the general result.

Semmelweis — “Etiology, Concept, and Prophylaxis of Childbed Fever” (1840s Vienna)

Question Why was maternal mortality from childbed fever far higher in the First Clinic (physicians & medical students) than the Second Clinic (midwives)?
Studies inside it (1) Observational comparison of the two clinics — a natural experiment, because admission alternated by day, not by the woman’s choice. (2) Observational comparisons that ruled out hypotheses: street births (unattended deliveries had low mortality), newborn deaths, seasonal/citywide patterns. (3) Small “experiments” changing one factor (lateral delivery position, priest’s route). (4) Intervention: chlorinated lime handwashing from mid-May 1847 — a before/after study; Clinic 1 mortality dropped to Clinic 2’s level.
Hypotheses rejected Epidemic “atmospheric-cosmic-telluric” influences (would hit both clinics and the city equally; street births were spared); overcrowding (Clinic 2 was more crowded); fear/shame/the priest’s bell; rough exams by foreign students; diet, ventilation, delivery position.
Two case redefinitions (1) Newborns who died had the same disease → childbed fever isn’t unique to postpartum women. (2) Kolletschka, a male colleague cut by a student’s scalpel during an autopsy, died of the same disease → the cause is cadaveric particles carried on hands. He later widened it to decaying organic matter from living patients (a discharging uterine cancer, a carious knee).
Why no jump in Table 6 around 1841 Table 6 is the whole hospital (both clinics combined); the 1840 reassignment only moved where the male students worked, so total exposure didn’t change — the gap shows up between clinics (Table 1). The big rise came earlier (~1823) when Vienna adopted autopsy-based anatomical teaching.
Paradigm shift From speculative/humoral/miasma explanations to anatomical-pathological medicine with a specific transmissible cause and numerical comparison — a step toward germ theory.
Context Women begged not to be sent to Clinic 1 and some delivered “on the street” to avoid it — so they knew. Poor and unmarried women came because care was free in exchange for serving as teaching subjects, and their infants could enter the foundling home.
If they could choose clinics Selection: SES, health, prior births, knowledge of reputation. If an obstetrician assigned them: provider selection (sicker/complicated cases to one clinic).
Practice calc RR Clinic 1 vs 2 = (1989/20,042) ÷ (691/17,791) = 2.56; Clinic 2 vs 1 = 0.39 (reciprocals). Odds of death in Clinic 1 = 1989/18,053 = 0.11.

Salk 1954 polio field trial

Vaccine Inactivated (formaldehyde-killed) poliovirus, injected → bloodstream antibodies → prevents paralysis, not intestinal infection. Contrast Sabin’s oral live-attenuated vaccine (gut immunity, rare vaccine-associated paralysis; US went IPV-only in 2000).
Design Two studies at once, ~1.8 million children. Observed-control areas: 2nd graders with consent vaccinated; all 1st & 3rd graders observed as controls. Placebo-control areas: consenting 1st–3rd graders randomized, double-blind to vaccine or placebo.
Why two The NFIP (Basil O’Connor) chose the observed-control plan as most acceptable to parents and states; scientists objected; Thomas Francis ran an independent placebo-controlled evaluation.
Design issues Observed-control is biased: vaccinated = volunteers vs controls = everyone (consenters + refusers); different grades/ages; no blinding → diagnostic bias. Consent was associated with higher SES, and paralytic polio was more common in higher-SES children (later age of infection) — so “not inoculated” children had lower rates than the placebo group. Polio is rare → enormous sample needed.
Big result Vaccine effective against paralytic polio (~60–90% depending on case definition); no significant effect on nonparalytic polio; the randomized placebo areas gave a higher, more valid efficacy estimate than the observed areas; all fatal cases were in controls. Lesson: randomization + blinding matter.

BNT162b2 (Pfizer/BioNTech) Covid-19 vaccine trial, NEJM 2020

Purposes Efficacy against confirmed Covid-19 and safety (reactogenicity, adverse events).
Design Multinational, randomized 1:1, placebo-controlled, observer-blinded Phase 2/3 trial; ~43,500 people ≥16; two doses 21 days apart. Phase 2/3 = seamless design — Phase 2 safety/immunogenicity in an initial subset rolling into the Phase 3 efficacy trial. Enrollment began late July 2020; data cutoff mid-November 2020.
vs. Salk placebo arm Similar: randomized, placebo, blinded, healthy volunteers, prevention trial, huge N because cases are rare. Different: individual randomization across countries, adults, symptom-triggered testing, event-driven timing, industry-funded, no parallel observational study.
Case definition Symptoms plus a positive lab (NAAT) test ≥7 days after dose 2 — symptom-based and etiology-confirmed. Alternatives: any infection incl. asymptomatic (needs routine testing of everyone; captures transmission) or severe disease only (too rare → bigger trial needed).
Two primary endpoints VE in those without prior infection, and in those with or without prior infection (prior infection could change apparent efficacy; real populations include both).
Stopping rule For vaccine-enhanced disease; one-sided because the concern runs in one direction only (more severe cases in the vaccine arm).
ITT / per protocol ITT = everyone randomized. Injections were given by staff, so nearly everyone received the intervention as assigned — little room for noncompliance to differ ITT from per-protocol.
Analysis VE from incidence rates (cases per 1,000 person-years of surveillance time); Figure 3 is a cumulative-incidence curve after dose 1.
Results ~95% efficacy; curves separate ~12 days after dose 1; reactions (injection-site pain, fatigue, headache) more common with vaccine, more after dose 2, mostly mild/moderate and brief.
Calc VE by person-time = 1 − (8/2.214)/(162/2.222) = 95.0%; by cumulative incidence = 1 − (8/17,411)/(162/17,511) = 95.0%. Nearly identical because follow-up time was similar in both arms.
Validity Internal: strong (randomized, blinded, large). External: short follow-up, rare adverse events undetectable, excluded pregnant/immunocompromised people. Long-term placebo comparison impossible because placebo recipients were offered vaccine after authorization.
Funding BioNTech and Pfizer — a potential conflict of interest worth noting when weighing the evidence.

LEAP — Randomized Trial of Peanut Consumption in Infants at Risk (NEJM 2015)

Descriptive motivation Peanut allergy ~10× more common in Jewish children in the UK than in Israel, where infants eat peanut snacks early; allergy rising despite avoidance advice.
Design Randomized, open-label (not blinded) behavioral/dietary prevention trial; 640 high-risk infants (4–11 months, severe eczema and/or egg allergy); two cohorts stratified by skin-prick test (negative vs. small wheal), each separately powered; consume vs avoid peanut until 60 months. Long trial → more loss to follow-up and nonadherence than a vaccine trial.
Outcome Peanut allergy by oral food challenge at 60 months (objective → partly offsets lack of blinding).
Design issues Infants with large wheals were excluded (likely already allergic). Consumption-arm infants who reacted at the baseline challenge stayed in the ITT analysis to preserve randomization. Worst-case imputation: assume all missing outcomes went against the hypothesis — if still significant, missing data can’t explain the result. Adherence was parent-reported.
Result Early consumption sharply reduced peanut allergy in both cohorts: relative reduction ≈ 86% (SPT-negative) and 70% (SPT-positive), computed exactly like vaccine efficacy. Changed infant-feeding guidelines.
Generalizability High-risk infants in the UK; not directly to low-risk infants or infants already allergic. Funded mainly by NIAID (NIH).

Framingham Enquiry (Dawber & Kannel)

Geographic, general-population prospective cohort started 1948 to study the natural history of cardiovascular disease. Why Framingham: town roster, near medical centers, cooperative before, “reasonably typical.” Random sample of 2/3 of adults 30–62 (69% response) plus volunteers, free of CVD at baseline, examined every 2 years. Analysis was simple — SMRs (standardized morbidity ratios) and 8-year cumulative incidence. Findings: hypertension, high cholesterol, ECG abnormalities, smoking, and obesity raise CHD risk, and combinations multiply it (the origin of “risk factors”). Key lesson in the discussion: an association does not prove that intervening on the factor will lower risk by the same amount (MRFIT later tested this and was null). External validity limited: one New England town, essentially no racial variation.

Aluminum-Adsorbed Vaccines and Chronic Diseases in Childhood (Danish nationwide cohort, Annals 2025)

Why aluminum Adjuvant that strengthens the immune response to non-live vaccines.
Design Historical (register-based) nationwide cohort; ~1.2 million children born in Denmark 1997–2018 who were alive and resident at age 2; registries linked by personal ID number.
Exposure Cumulative aluminum (mg) received from vaccines in the first 2 years of life.
Outcomes 50 chronic conditions (autoimmune, atopic/allergic, neurodevelopmental incl. autism and ADHD) diagnosed from age 2 to age 5 (extended follow-up in sensitivity analyses).
Natural experiment Exposure varied mainly because the national vaccine schedule/products changed over calendar time, not because of parents’ choices → less confounding by family health behaviors.
Exclusion logic Emigration/death in first 2 years (exposure window and follow-up incomplete); congenital/preexisting conditions (could change both vaccination and outcome risk); implausibly many aluminum vaccinations (data errors); mothers not in Denmark before birth (missing maternal covariates); missing covariates (complete-case analysis); outcome already present by age 2 (prevalent cases — must be at risk at start of follow-up, and prevents reverse causation).
Why survival analysis Follow-up varies and is censored (emigration, death, end of data); Cox regression handles time-to-diagnosis and confounder adjustment → hazard ratios.
Confounders adjusted Sex, calendar year & season of birth, preterm birth, birthweight, GP visits before 2, maternal age, birthplace, smoking, health conditions, prescriptions, household income. Calendar year is a strong one: tied to exposure (schedule changes) and outcome (diagnostic trends like rising autism diagnoses).
Figure 3 Adjusted HR per 1-mg increase with 95% CIs for each outcome. Log scale because ratios are symmetric on a log scale (2 and 0.5 equidistant from 1). CI widths differ because rarer outcomes have fewer cases → less precision. No outcome showed a significant increase; a few estimates were slightly below 1.
Misclassification Non-differential exposure misclassification usually biases toward the null for a binary exposure — not guaranteed in every situation, and a concern for a null study because it could mask a real effect. Registry vaccination data are fairly accurate.
Critiques (section discussion) Judge critiques by whether they identify a mechanism that could hide a true effect (very few children had zero aluminum; follow-up to age 5 is short for some diagnoses; residual confounding) versus claims about motives, which aren’t evidence about validity either way.

8 Fresh practice problems

New numbers, same skills. Do them closed-book with a calculator. Answers are in Answer_Keys.md.

P1. Occurrence. A town has 10,000 people on Jan 1; 200 already have disease X. During the year, 150 new cases occur and 30 people with X die of it. (a) Prevalence on Jan 1? (b) One-year cumulative incidence? (c) Cause-specific mortality per 100,000 (use 10,000 as the population)? (d) Case-fatality among everyone who had X during the year?

P2. Person-time. Five people are followed: A for 2 years (no event), B for 3 years (event), C for 1.5 years (lost to follow-up), D for 4 years (no event), E for 0.5 years (event). Incidence rate per 100 person-years?

P3. Direct age adjustment. Population A: ages 0–39 = 8,000 people/16 deaths; 40–64 = 6,000/30; 65+ = 2,000/40. Population B: 0–39 = 3,000/3; 40–64 = 4,000/16; 65+ = 5,000/75. Standard population: 10,000 / 10,000 / 5,000. (a) Crude death rates per 1,000. (b) Age-adjusted rates per 1,000. (c) Which population has higher mortality, and why do crude and adjusted disagree?

P4. SMR. A factory has 4,000 workers aged 25–44 and 1,000 aged 45–64. National death rates are 1 per 1,000 and 6 per 1,000 in those age groups. The factory had 18 deaths. Expected deaths? SMR? Interpret.

P5. Outbreak. At a picnic: potato salad — 60 ate (45 ill), 40 didn’t (6 ill). Chicken — 80 ate (40 ill), 20 didn’t (11 ill). Attack rates, RRs, likely vehicle, odds of illness among potato-salad eaters.

P7. Screening and prevalence. A test has Se 90% and Sp 95%. (a) Build the 2×2 for 2,000 people with 5% prevalence; give PPV and NPV. (b) Repeat with 20% prevalence. (c) What happened and why?

P8. Cohort measures. 1,000 smokers: 40 develop disease in 5 years. 4,000 non-smokers: 40 develop disease. Find RR, AR, AR%, PAR, PAR%. Interpret AR% and PAR% in one sentence each.

P9. Efficacy & NNT. Vaccine trial: placebo arm 10,000 (200 cases), vaccine arm 10,000 (40 cases). VE? NNT?

P10. Case-control. 100 cases and 200 controls; 60 cases and 50 controls were exposed. OR? Interpret. Can you compute the RR?

P11. Confounding or interaction? (a) Crude RR = 3.0; age < 50 stratum RR = 1.4; age ≥ 50 RR = 1.5. (b) Crude RR = 2.5; men RR = 1.0; women RR = 5.0.

P12. Herd immunity. R0 = 4? R0 = 12?

P13. Kappa. Two radiologists read 200 films. A calls 60 positive; B calls 80 positive. Both positive on 50; both negative on 110. Observed agreement, chance agreement, κ?

P14. RR confidence interval. Exposed: 20 cases among 100. Unexposed: 10 among 100. RR and 95% CI (use the formula sheet). Significant?

P15. Design ID. Name the design: (a) Records from 1980s employment files identify asbestos-exposed and unexposed workers; death certificates through 2020 are linked. (b) Children with leukemia and children without are asked about past pesticide exposure. (c) 24 schools are randomized to a curriculum or usual teaching. (d) Countries’ average salt intake is correlated with stroke mortality. (e) Migraine patients get drug then placebo (or reverse), with a washout between.
